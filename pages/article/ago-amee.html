Ago Amee


by Mahogany L. Browne



Ago Amee: a West African phrase, a call and response, comes from the Akan people of Ghana. This communal action is where the neat margins of a grant report can stifle the museum walls with hard questions, like: Whose land was this before a flag named it? How much of our humanity was lost? And how do we decolonize this place if we’ve lost sight of the stars?

Call and Response is an emergency system, not a performance. This is what happens when someone says, “I am here,” and the room responds, “We are here with you.”

Call-and-response asks listeners to bear some of the weight of the truth. The heavy insistence that we ain’t going anywhere without our liberation attached.

Call. And. Response. It’s how we find each other through the smoke and noise. It is the body saying, “I cannot hold this alone.” There is an emotional structure to how a call creates space, and a response fills it without changing its original feeling. Following this rhythm—your voice, then mine, then yours—teaches us to take turns and can help lessen fear. There is a physical manifestation that reveals: shoulders lowering, breath moving, jaws unclenching; the nervous system learning in real time. The body serves as a witness and an echo.

That reply, right there, is the miracle of miracles.

The truth is, the way stories are gathered in this country often resembles a raid. There is a sudden exposure, lights on, names taken, context stripped, voices ignored. The person(s) remain in the wreckage of their own disclosure, while the mandated reporter files a copy and moves past the carnage.

Call-and-response becomes a tourniquet and a microphone. We do not believe in raids. We believe in building safe passage. We do not believe in raids. We believe in creating sanctuaries.

Call-and-response is a lifeline. It provides support and unity in struggle. We accuse lobbyists of supporting genocide through policies that cause suffering for profit. This includes state-sanctioned violence, the war on drugs, the school-to-prison pipeline, and the maternal death rates of Black and Brown women. Structural violence relies on the lie that lack of resources and inequities of the justice system are as inevitable as the weather, rather than the result of human decisions.

Counter-narrative is the refusal of that lie. It means naming the larger social and institutional structures as "systems"—entities that are intentionally designed, maintained, defended, funded, and normalized. It insists that what is manmade may also be unmade. But we cannot be merely oppositional; if all we do is speak back to the wound, we risk shaping our entire conversation around the injury and becoming fluent only in describing what hurts us.

Call & Response is a healing initiative. 'Healing,' meaning fostering restoration and well-being. The initiative invites the most marginalized and silenced voices—those systematically excluded or ignored—to the center of the room to be held sacred, not hash-tagged.

Healing stories teach the strategy and choreography of staying alive. They are evidence that harm did not wear away our softness, nor did it eliminate our ability to feel joy, anger, and hope. Healing is political. It is a vote, a mutual aid spreadsheet, a communal childcare rotation, and a quiet practice of calling someone back.

And yes, healing stories can be angry and hilarious. Healing knows prayer and faith have their own pair of shoes, because healing is well aware that grief has a backbone. And somewhere, a body is reading the headline as a threat. Somewhere, a child is overhearing language that should come with a hand to hold. And somewhere, someone is learning how to brace themselves as a default posture. Because information without care can cause harm, a story without consent becomes theft, and speed without a relationship becomes a weapon. Imagine that. Call and Response is instead a revolutionary humanitarian act!

Call us forward with zines and well-informed journalism that return the focus to the people, building a practice of alarm clock and protest chant. We must respond: we bridge, we water, we clinic. We are more than decoration; we are infrastructure.

Call and response is a stand against soft colonialism, the latter a process of erasing community vernacular by translating vibrant, lived speech into a standardized narrative that echoes with no one's authentic voice.

Call and response is a powerful tool because it holds the whole truth: violence, tenderness, history, anger, breath, recipes, grief, and laughter. We do it to narrate ourselves, together. Our healing is an evolution. We value this connection. We will no longer be ignored.
