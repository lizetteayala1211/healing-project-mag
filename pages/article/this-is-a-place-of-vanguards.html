“This is a Place of Vanguards”: A Portrait of the South Bronx


by Liza Austria, Grant Aumell, Nieves Ayress, Vicky Ayress, Melissa Barber, Walter Bosque, Libertad Guerra, Mychal Johnson, and Monxo López



It was a Tuesday in July when the Young Lords took to 234 E 149th Street. Built on the organization’s commitment to the liberation of the Puerto Rican people, their chapter in New York had taken to organizing both against the city’s structural disinvestments in the South Bronx, and around the health of their neighbors and land. And so, in an act of refusal that followed a history of medicinal malpractice and a systemic lack of sanitation, nutrition, and healthcare in the area, their latest campaign took root on this early summer morning in 1970 by way of the Lincoln Hospital takeover.

Over 50 years later, South Bronx residents are still repairing the violence perpetuated by the medical industrial complex, compounded by that of warehouse and real estate developments, fossil fuels, power plants, and waste transfer stations. South Bronx Unite/The Land Stewards have a vision for a neighborhood where community members have equal access to clean air and nutritious food, affordable healthcare and homes, green spaces, and an environment that centers their dignity. In response, they have activated a collective in pursuit of local, community-designed solutions that span the Mott Haven-Port Morris Waterfront Plan, which prioritizes public access to and engagement with the shoreline; the Mott Haven-Port Morris Community Land Stewards, which acquires and preserves land to resist mass displacement; and H.E.Arts, a long-fought-for community center integrating the diverse health, education, and arts dreams of the many who realized it.

What follows is a reflective portrait of this specific place across time. Derived from various interviews with South Bronx neighbors—from former members of the Young Lords to founding members of South Bronx Unite/The Land Stewards—this excerpt intertwines the people’s insights on their land, their health, and their history as if they were narrating around a kitchen table.

Melissa: I was born and raised here in the South Bronx. I tell people, “This is the best place on the planet and I will fight you if you think otherwise.” I love this community. I love the history of who we are, that you'll always hear the music popping at the loudest volume no matter what time it is.

Libertad: This [is] a place of vanguards, so many intersectional legacies—from Theatro Puerto Rico in the Mambo craze to Fashion Moda, which [started as] a downtown movement. It was the birthplace of intersections between graffiti and music and many other things.

Liza: The neighborhood is the birthplace of hip hop and salsa. It has this rich cultural legacy, vibrancy, and [yet] the resources have been stripped.

Nieves: Todo lo poquito que hay aquí en el Sur del Bronx ha sido producto de la lucha de la comunidad. Aquí nada nos han dado gratis. Cada parque, cada jardín, cada edificio nuevo es fruto del trabajo, del esfuerzo, de la organización de la gente. Nosotros peleamos por todo, desde el agua caliente hasta el aire limpio. Y seguimos peleando, porque si uno no defiende su barrio, nadie lo hace.

Translation: Everything that exists here in the South Bronx has been born of the people’s struggle. Nothing has ever been given to us for free. Every park, every garden, every new building is the product of community work, effort, and organization. We’ve had to fight for everything—from hot water to clean air. And we keep fighting, because if you don’t defend your neighborhood, no one will.

Monxo: A lot of the environmental racism that happened here was urbicide. And the consequence of that violence is still with us—the health pressures, both mental and physical, that we have in our communities. I’ve caught myself thinking, what have I done wrong? I haven’t, but that’s the effect of this systemic undermining of communities.

Mychal: The start of [our organization], South Bronx Unite, came from that cycle of harm. [We were] trying to stop Fresh Direct, the online grocery, from coming here. They're [now] sited on a Native American burial ground, on public land. And they [were able to do] that based off of an environmental impact report that was more than 20 years old. (Editor’s Note: Fresh Direct alone sees approximately 1,000 diesel truck trips a day).

Libertad: We started seeing the double standards that communities like [ours] confronted, how environmental impact studies were mandated for neighborhoods just two train stops away from us; yet ours was 20 years old. That was an awakening.

Mychal: We [said], we need something different. And if you don't know what it looks like, we'll create it ourselves. Just get with us. Healing is ever present, and it’s important to uplift the history as well as carry it on.

I live on the block and didn't really realize [its history] until my mother visited. She had sciatica. I knew they were doing acupuncture down the street and I took her there, asked for treatment, and met people who were working there. My mother was involved with the Black Panthers in Chicago, and the Young Lords originated from Chicago. I realized how important what happened there [was]: that act of self-determination, them taking on Lincoln Hospital, creating this treatment for recovery because they're tired [of] people suffering, [of] services not being provided for people here.

Walter: [In the seventies], I was in nursing school. My oldest friend, Mickey Melendez, was one of the founders of the Young Lords. I ran into him after years of not seeing him. He says, “Well, we just took over Lincoln Hospital, why don't you come volunteer?” The next day I reported to the auditorium, which they had set up as a clinic, and the rest is history.

We were doing methadone detox, but we realized that the methadone wasn't really doing much for our community. It was very toxic. We were looking for an alternative. Mutulu Shakur came into one of our meetings with a newspaper article from Bangkok, Thailand. An acupuncturist there was treating a client, who confessed that he had been smoking opioid since he was a teenager; but since he started taking the acupuncture treatments for whatever ailment he had, he had no desire to smoke. We looked at each other and said, “Let's try it.” So we hired two acupuncturists and were assigned to be their assistants. We had to explain to the clients that we were gonna put needles in their body and they weren't gonna get high. We trained each other as a regular therapy. And we started detoxing people with acupuncture.

Four years into the program, we got invited to the World Health Acupuncture Conference. They took a liking to us when they heard our story and offered us six scholarships to go to their institute in Montreal. We went up there for three years and became the first non-Asian acupuncturists in Canada. We opened up a school in Lincoln [Hospital], and we started treating not only the clients, but their families. That person detoxing has to go home and deal with his family. So we invited everybody to come and try it. They loved it. Of course, the hospital didn't like it. The city didn't like it.

Mychal: Land has been used to cause assaults on health and wellbeing and the futures of the community. You name the ailment, we're on the top of the chart: infant mortality rates, asthma, BC diabetes, cardiovascular disease—a lot of which is due to lack of either green space or too much built environment. We handle all of the Bronx’s waste here in Mott Haven—heavy diesel trucks and phospho power plants, three highways and five bridges that intersect with us. How can you not have the health outcomes that we're dealing with?

Libertad: We founded our community Land Trust in 2013. [It was] about environmental justice, the land, and how you develop places not for one person or one entity—it's for public good, for the collective. We started scanning all the empty lots, all the forgotten waterfronts, all the things that are supposed to be public land that aren't public lands to build in city accountability.

Monxo: I am a cartographer. I think it's healthy and insightful to think about problems not only [as] attached to communities and to people, but as being attached to the land. Even our new gentrifying neighbors, they're gonna breathe the same air we breathe. They're gonna be surrounded by cops just like we are, [though] maybe the cops don't behave towards them the same way. I think that that's a good exercise to break from the thought that I suspect visits all of us: The problem, it's me. The problem is my people.

Libertad: I don't want a revolution if I can't dance in it. (That's Emma Goldman). This process is gonna really take you into a whole lot of technical domains that are very weary, from zoning to law to bureaucracy. So if you don't have the communal aspect of it—that's what reminds you why you're doing it—you will burn out. We got arrested [on] the waterfront. And we did it all with a joyful cultural lens, because we believe that protest needs to be artistic and that's what gets us through. Our methodology [is] one of togetherness, one of visioning, one of joy.

Vicky: Lo que quieren son cosas decorativas, cosas que no molesten. Pero nosotros molestamos. Nuestros murales hablan de lo que la gente vive: del hambre, de la pandemia, de la tierra, del derecho a existir aquí. No pintamos para adornar paredes, pintamos para que la comunidad se vea, para que piense, para que se respete. Porque el arte también es una forma de protesta, una forma de cuidar la memoria del barrio.

Translation: What they want are decorative things—things that don’t disrupt anyone. But we disrupt them. Our murals speak about what people live through: hunger, the pandemic, the land, the right to exist here. We don’t paint to decorate walls, we paint so the community can see itself, think, and be respected. Because art, too, is a form of protest—a way of caring for the neighborhood’s memory.

Mychal: Celebrating, recreating, having our people and our children at the waters edge. Our end goal is healthier outcomes for our children, for our community, so we can have what everyone else has. [It’s] not like we want what they’ve got, but we should have access to our peninsula. Like the community garden, [it] was built out of necessity. Folks said “we need a place to bring people together”.

Grant: [When I moved here], the first place I went was the community garden to find my people And I found them. [I love] when the kids come in and they have a chance to run around. We'll have fires [and] kids [will] make a s’more for the first time, they're like, “I've never done this before!” Those experiences are real—digging a hole, finding a worm, poking a fire, seeing butterflies.

Walter: I've been teaching Tai Chi here, one of the ancient martial arts, for medicinal purposes. What can be more natural than exercise and breathing?

Grant: Jose, he lives across the street from the garden and keeps an eye on it. He’s a DJ, so we have a sound system and music. What's great is people will come by and he'll call them in and be like, “I've known this guy since 1972!” I'm glad he's there 'cause he can provide a personal history. I appreciate that. It gives me a sense of what was here before I arrived.

Vicky: En los jardines comunitarios es donde de verdad hacemos comunidad. Ahí la gente se conoce, comparte comida, comparte historias. Una trae su plato de Turquía, otra su sazón del Caribe, otra su receta de África. A través de la comida y de lo que se siembra creamos comunidad, una comunidad más saludable y más humana.

Translation: In the community gardens, that’s where we truly build community. People meet there, share food, share stories. One brings a dish from Turkey, another their Caribbean seasoning, another a recipe from Africa. Through food and through what we plant, we create community—a healthier, more human one.

Monxo: I participate in jams in the community garden. This is a very musical community. The Puerto Rican people, my people, lived here. Dominicans, people from Honduras, from Mexico, people from West Africa. Very musical people. It’s something we still practice one way or another. I like playing guitar on my stoop, just being out there. Sometimes people will request a song. The other day, a young lady coming home from college break, Fatima, stopped and started talking to me about the election and how she’s studying political science. Her family comes from Ghana, and she was telling me that she wants to come back from college and hopefully be a council member. I've known for years that if I have the guitar and I'm playing there, people are gonna stop and they're gonna talk to me about all sorts of things.

Liza: The music making that is happening there, the community—it can be this healing hub.

Melissa: [We now can] have a space…to create this wholeness and live off the history that was first here—and also transition into this new generational legacy.

Walter: This is the neighborhood where [alternatives] came from. [In the seventies], we got a taste of the cultural revolution. We were so-called radicals. I didn't realize I was radical until they put me on the list: Mayor Koch and Schumer [published] a report accusing us of doing everything but killing somebody. It was a lie, but they got rid of us. They kept the doctors, but excommunicated 14 of us out of the acupuncture program. They transferred us to other municipal hospitals who had drug programs, but we couldn't do acupuncture, we couldn't organize. The doctor that stayed behind, the late Michael Smith, did the best he could. In 1985 he incorporated The National Acupuncture Detoxification Association. But he forgot to mention who the pioneers were. He got all the credit, fame, and fortune. But it was a bunch of new radicals in the South Bronx who brought us here.

Nieves: “El trabajo de base es el corazón de todo. Hay que hacerlo persona a persona, cuadra a cuadra, hasta integrar a todo el mundo, hasta el gato y el perro. A eso le llamamos el poder popular: cuando la gente se reconoce como dueña de su destino, no como víctima. Si no tienes cultura, arte, y educación, el pueblo se destruye. Por eso seguimos, aunque cansadas, aunque viejas, porque la lucha también es alegría.”

Translation: Grassroots work is the heart of everything. You have to do it person by person, block by block, until everyone’s included—even the cats and dogs. That’s what we call people’s power: when the community recognizes itself as the author of its destiny, not its victim. Without culture, art, and education, a people falls apart. That’s why we keep going, even tired, even old—because the struggle is also joy.

Melissa: We’re the [new] stewards of our own destinies. Our creed is No Mal. Do No Harm. That's in the spiritual DNA. That's who we are. There is a history that people try to cover up and hide [here]. I always remember if you know the truth of who you are and your identity, you'll step into it. Everything that we do is to create wholeness and wellness without harm. All of us collectively will fall in line to do no harm in whatever area we're tasked to.
