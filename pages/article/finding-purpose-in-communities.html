Finding Purpose in Communities


by Pamela A. Smart


When I was younger, I considered communities to be distinguished by geography. I suppose outsiders might describe us as a “community” of incarcerated people. That may be true in a general sense, but after 35 years in here, I’ve found that real community is forged in more sacred and intimate ways.

I’m in my fifteenth term as a grievance representative, elected by my peers every six months to help resolve their disputes or represent them at disciplinary hearings. One woman came to me to share that her son was taken away from her and put into foster care. She couldn’t see him as much and, when he did visit, it was supervised. She wanted to talk about writing a grievance requesting privacy with her son.

That was a year ago. Over time, she just felt comfortable with me, working through her feelings about the whole situation. She would come once a month—or sometimes a week, sometimes twice a day depending on what she was going through. A few months ago, she came to me with a big smile on her face and told me that her son is now being placed back in her family’s care. She wanted me to be the first to know, and we cried together. I held her happiness the way I held her sadness for so long. I think it was critical for her. I know it was critical for me.

On the same day she had come to me with her joy, I had been struggling. It was during count, and I don’t know why, but I started thinking about my father. He passed away at the end of June. I was coming down because the prison called movement line—you have to go, whether you're crying, whether you're grieving. Afterwards, I went to my office to put my stuff down. That’s when I saw this woman who shared the news about her son. At that moment, I needed to hear her happiness to recognize my own purpose. 20 minutes before that, I was feeling like I just didn't have one.

I'm not a licensed psychologist, nor a social worker. People always say, “Pam, I want to talk to you.” Sometimes, when they get into it, I’ll recommend they talk to someone at the Office of Mental Health (OMH). The response is a broken record: “I don't want to talk to them because I don't know them” or, “I don't trust them, I trust you.” In some cases, those who do have access to therapy only see their practitioner once a month, and that’s not enough. These therapists should be more visible, they should walk through the housing units and just talk to people. What’s more, the Department of Corrections needs to take people like me and give us some kind of training.

COVID was when that really hit me, that we really are our sister's keepers. We were on an island, like we had leprosy or something. Mental health crises were happening every day, all day. We had to take care of each other through locked doors, talk from cell to cell down the corridor, pass letters back and forth, fish things underneath the door to one another, check in when we didn't hear from somebody, and talk out of the window to get information to people in other units. I kept my radio on ABC News, and I would yell down the hall to give everyone the update. People were making masks out of bandanas, or walking around with clear plastic gloves on. The prison said that was misuse of state property. We couldn't even protect ourselves in the smallest ways without fear of getting misbehavior reports. When we woke up one day to a mask mandate, people started getting misbehavior reports for not having a mask on. At that point, I realized we were on our own. I realized it's not a one-size-fits-all here. You have to be able to adapt in your caregiving, because the method one day might not be the method the next.

It used to be that every unit went to the yard together, but during COVID, they separated the units. I remember thinking it’s never going to go back to what it was. And that’s exactly what happened.

Prison, by its very nature, strips us of our possessions and familial relationships. This leaves a void, and it’s on us to fill it. We have aunties and grandmas and sisters in here. When somebody's calling you their sister or looking at you like you're their Auntie or their Nana, those relationships are important. There’s an inherent responsibility. You’re repairing damage that was done to a person beforehand, and I don’t take that lightly. People say to me, “I’m 40 years old and I’ve never had a friend like you.” I have to remember all the time that this person or these people are watching, so what am I modeling?

I’ve been a leader and facilitator for my fellow Protestants for decades and, through my faith, I seek to model comfort and acceptance with words of encouragement I share at our weekly services. I find that our members recognize how loss can be compensated by community. How we can fill hearts and minds with support and love. How we hold new members in their weakest moments. Through my academic pursuits, I model vision and focus, like when Pell Grants were eliminated for college students in prisons years ago. I was part of a group of creative thinkers who convened various universities to generate funding for incarcerated students. We became a fierce force of resistance, resulting in the College Bound program that still exists today. Whether theological or academic, this work binds us together, providing the most powerful antidote to depression and despair: hope. Hope is the very air that incarcerated individuals need to breathe in order to survive.

My community has always furnished me with hope, carrying me through some of my most heartbreaking moments, providing light in dark spaces. When appeals have been denied or commutation requests summarily dismissed, my community has been instrumental to my survival. Most recently, when my beloved father passed away, my friends rallied by my side to provide support and solace. It was they who dried my tears, prayed with me, gave me beautiful and heartfelt cards, cooked special dishes to provide comfort food, and even cried with me.

Incarcerated people end up stepping into roles that we might not feel like we have the training to fill, but because of our lived experience, we actually are better trained than some of the professionals here. The old adage, “there is power in numbers,” rings true when communities invest in a common purpose, amplify the vision and voices of their people, and care about one another. I find that my cup is filled when I’m giving, and my healing happens when I'm helping with somebody else's healing.

As the years go on, though, I find myself tired in spirit and mind. This past November, the prison instituted a new policy eliminating all indoor recreation on certain housing units. This is where women gather to study, befriend one another, watch a movie or enjoy a meal together. It’s where people rely on each other when they receive bad news. It’s where mutual healing happens. But the new deputy of security wants everyone locked in their cells when they are not attending programs. No one can even take a shower beforehand—not even the mess hall workers who serve our food. People are suffering mental health breakdowns and sleeping all the time. We are all devastated. They claim they are doing this to decrease the violence. That’s what they said after COVID, too. But this is not the answer. We don’t even have access to the one space where we can hug one another. Morale is at an all-time low, and I am swamped with grievances.

I went to a unit the other day and announced, "Grievance on rounds! This is Pam.” People came running, yelling from their various units, “Pam, come to 12! Come to 11!” I couldn’t even hear. When I got back to my cell, I had to shed some of my clothes just to process the overwhelm. I keep asking myself: How am I going to provide the care I typically do within these confines? Am I qualified for this? Am I supposed to come up with these solutions? Why are mental health practitioners not visiting the locked-down units?

No matter how many fences or obstacles or blockades they build, love is going to make it through—and I am one of the keepers of that love. I am going to care for my peers in spite of it all. I’m not going to let darkness engulf us.
