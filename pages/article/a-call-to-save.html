A Call to Save Our Schools


by Brotherhood Sister Sol



A student was suspended for using a cell phone to call his parents during a fire drill.

Several students of Muslim faith were suspended for dress code violations, like wearing hijabs or jilbabs that were not the correct, school-approved shade.

Still others routinely wake up at 5 AM to wait over an hour in lines stretching outside of their schools before going through metal detectors and having their personal items searched. Despite arriving to school—not on time, but early—young people are made late to class. It is not uncommon for teachers or staff to penalize them, nor for young people to be issued a detention or suspension if they are considered argumentative or aggressive when challenging this punishment. Our young people often share being surveilled by cameras, digitally monitored on their computers, and wanded down multiple times a week.

New York City students are policed at least 100% more than the entire city populations of Jacksonville and San Diego. The Division of School Safety agents that interact with them daily are part of the New York Police Department (NYPD), trained in a 17-week program focused on behavioral threat assessment, armed assailant response, martial arts, substance abuse, human trafficking, emergency response, and detaining. (The investment in school safety officers and police has never proven to be effective at preventing or stopping school shootings or misbehavior; it has instead increased the number of gun-related offenses in schools and in-school and out-of-school suspensions).

It is clear to our youth that their schools do not trust them. The message they receive is that the adults in power—principals, teachers, staff, and parents—explicitly and subtly are willing to criminalize them before they even walk through school doors. Punitive practices where staff resort to suspensions, expulsions, police referrals, and arrests overwhelmingly target students of color, disabled youth, and LGBTQ students.

While our young people feel increasingly less safe in schools, security and police are trained to perceive our children as threats.

Still, many schools do not have social workers or a full-time, dedicated therapist on staff. And those that do encounter backlash. Mental health professionals are tasked to create meaningful relationships with hundreds of students, and yet other staff feel that the “disruptive” students are simply getting their way by “getting out of class” or receiving an undeserved “break.”

We so often hear and tell children that they are “the future.” If we truly believe this, it is our responsibility to invest in our most precious asset.

In our work at Brotherhood Sister Sol, we invite challenging conversations, holding young people accountable to their own definitions of sisterhood, brotherhood, and leadership. We accompany them long-term—not to call them out on their shortcomings, but to bring them in with love. Our aim is to educate, guide, and allow them to be children, which includes making mistakes. This work demands dedicating years to accompany a young person, to walk by their side and develop meaningful relationships built on love and trust.

We’ve asked our young people to reflect on what safety is, who keeps them safe, and how they are kept safe. They are the solution, they have spoken loudly, and they have given us very clear answers. Our youth organizers’ Save Our Schools Campaign outlines new pathways for students:

Our young people want wellness centers to be required in all NYC public schools, with implementation beginning in schools in most need of mental health support. They speak about the need for “a dedicated space for students to nurture their mental, emotional, and physical well-being, helping us thrive in school environments that too often feel limiting and oppressive.”

Our young people want an end to arrests. They seek alternatives for infractions, including professional mental health staff, restorative justice programs, and peer-to-peer mediation. They cite several cities across the country that have shifted from having security in schools to expanded counseling programs; and school boards in smaller jurisdictions who have terminated contracts with their police departments.

Our young people want licensure support and financial and academic incentives for more people of color to pursue careers in school counseling and therapy. Our students want to see the faces, colors, and cultures that make up the city reflected back to them as they seek support.

Our young people want wellness centers with a 1:100 ratio of support staff to students. This means that one counselor, social worker, and therapist should be assigned to no more than 100 students. (The National Association of Social Workers and the American School Counselor Association have recommended 1 counselor and 1 social worker for every 250 students, with 1 counselor for every 50 students who require the most intensive care. The national average falls somewhere between 1:400 students).

Our young people want to redirect funds from the Division of School Safety to wellness centers, safety resources, staffing, and peer-to-peer and restorative justice programs. The outcomes of these preventative measures point towards high graduation rates and more retention.

We must listen to young people’s lived experiences. We must feel a sense of urgency to investigate how our young people are navigating this world.
