Letter from the Healing Project



I started The Healing Project in an effort to speak directly to the many damages caused by our society’s systems. Our political, medical, legal, educational, and cultural systems claim to help us, yet often intersect to make communities vulnerable to harm. It feels essential in this moment that these communities are able to organize, strategize, and support each other.

Inspired by my mentor Anna Deavere Smith’s deep listening practice, I have spent over a decade with people across the country exploring the daily realities of violence, incarceration, detention, and policing alongside the care strategies that emerge from these same communities. What began as an art project has evolved into an arts organization that addresses structural violence through artistic works, collective healing spaces, and advocacy initiatives. We envision our creative works as portals to another world: one where those who are currently oppressed in our society are recognized as the very people who hold the wisdom we need to build better futures. The answers we claim to be searching for are right here, right now. Which is what brings us to The Healing Project Magazine.

This magazine is a record.A container for truth-telling, this is a place where we will amplify those doing the daily work to fight structural violence and build collective care.

This magazine is an offeringIn a media landscape that often centers despair, we strive to be an antidote: a reminder that people all around us are already shaping the world we want to live in, and that we must also orient ourselves around solutions.

This magazine is an invitation.Faced with a world that siloes and silences, we want to provide a space that encourages complexity, welcomes vulnerability, and builds community.

The inaugural issue of The Healing Project Magazine is grounded in the theme of Call and Response, emerging in tandem with our exhibition of the same name at the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA). Rooted in the Black Radical Tradition, call and response is a technique and a polyrhythmic feedback network, emerging from traditions of making within the African diaspora.

We root ourselves in this practice in real time, applying call and response both as a musical and organizing framework. This means asking people what they need and responding directly to those needs through artistic practice. It also means making abolitionist work that counters stereotypical and harmful narratives. Within the framework of call and response, everything is relational—from our interview-based creative question practices to our healing rooms to our performance work and beyond.

As part of our work to develop this exhibition and my role as a 2025 MoMA Adobe Creative Resident, The Healing Project partnered with four organizations across New York to create artistic works that stem from the same interview-based process that sparked this project over 10 years ago. These collaborations also became extensions of our Healing Rooms: spaces where we can gather, listen, and respond to what our communities most need.

With The Fortune Society—a reentry organization in Queens that also promotes alternatives to incarceration—we’ve co-created a photography series titled Who Gets to Name You?. The visual collaboration asks who holds the power to define identity, and what it means to reclaim that naming for oneself. We also have composed a mixtape incorporating seven artists from Fortune whose songs span themes of spirituality to free association as a means of self-expression.

With Parole Prep—an organization that advocates for the release of people serving life sentences—we will present We Heal Us and Spin The Yard. Both are sonic explorations of how people in prison care and show up for one another, exposing the Department of Corrections’ negligence while highlighting mutual aid practices of incarcerated people.

With South Bronx Unite/The Land Stewards—a coalition dedicated to the social, environmental, and economic future of Mott Haven and Port Morris—we developed a documentary project titled Still Standing. Through interviews with artistic and political leaders, we trace the neighborhood’s artistic and organizing history, situating them within the lineage of the Young Lords’ “Lincoln Detox” community health program and the community’s rebuilding of this effort.

With Brotherhood Sister Sol—a Harlem-based organization providing holistic and long-term support services to young people aged 8-22—we co-wrote a short film with high school students in the Liberation Program. The fictional piece is grounded in students’ lived experiences with escalating school suspensions while providing solutions they have explored like wellness centers in schools.

These works will be on display at MoMA for our community day. There, organization collaborators and their loved ones will have the opportunity to sit with the work, provide feedback, and consider how these pieces might live on to activate others outside of MoMA’s walls.

In this issue, you’ll find reflections and offerings from artists, activists, and other members of these organizations, alongside calls to action and invitations to join their work. The issue also features a moving piece by local community-builder and artist Mahogany L. Browne, and a closing prayer by Sue Ariza, a beloved member of The Healing Project team.

The revolution will not be televised, it will happen in community centers and living rooms. We hope this magazine will be a place where beauty, tenderness, imagination, refusal, dignity, and process coalesce to reveal what’s possible when healing takes precedent.

In solidarity,
Samora Pinderhughes
Artistic & Executive Director, The Healing Project
